Locked Out of Your Own Google Business Profile? Here's How to Get It Back

September 8, 2026 · 5 min read · by Chris Kelley

It is not a tech problem. It is a keys problem.

Somebody has the keys to your business on Google, and it is not you.

I get this call a couple times a month, and it almost always starts the same way. Someone tries to update their hours, or fix a phone number that has been wrong since March, or add photos from a job they are actually proud of — and Google tells them they do not have permission. On their own business.

How this happens to normal people

Nobody loses their Google listing on purpose. It is almost always one of these four:

  • The marketing company you fired. They set it up in 2021 as part of a package, they still hold it, and nobody thought to ask about it on the way out.

  • The employee who left. He created it on his personal Gmail because he was the one holding the phone that afternoon. He has since moved to Denver.

  • The guy who built your website. Claiming the listing was step nine of his onboarding checklist. You never saw the login.

  • Your own old email. The one from two phones ago that you cannot get into anymore. This one stings the most, because technically you do own it.

None of these are your fault. All of them are your problem.

Why this is urgent and not just annoying

When somebody looks for a plumber, a roofer, or a water treatment guy, they open Maps, look at the three businesses Google shows them, glance at the star ratings, and call one. That is the whole decision. It takes about eight seconds and your website barely factors into it.

Now think about running that gauntlet with a listing you cannot touch.

Your hours are wrong and you cannot fix them. Somebody left a bad review and you cannot respond to it. A customer sent a message through the profile three weeks ago and you have never seen it. Google added a "temporarily closed" label because nobody has logged in and confirmed otherwise.

Every one of those is a job walking out the door, and none of them show up anywhere you would think to look.

Step 1: Find out who actually has it

Search your business name on Google and click your listing. Look for "Own this business?" or "Claim this business."

When you start the claim, Google will tell you it is already managed by somebody — and it will show you a masked version of that person's email. Something like j••••@gmail.com.

That masking looks useless. It is not. Nine times out of ten it is enough to tell you exactly who you are dealing with. Four dots after a j, and you parted ways with a guy named Jason two years ago? There is your answer.

Write it down. You need it for step three.

Step 2: Request access through Google

Google walks you through a short form. Fill it out and it emails whoever currently holds the listing. They get seven days to respond.

Three things can happen:

  • They approve it. Best case. Sometimes same day.

  • They deny it. Annoying, but not the end. You can appeal, and you will need documentation that ties you to the business.

  • They ignore it. This is by far the most common outcome, and it is actually fine.

Step 3: Skip the form and call the person

This is the step everybody skips, and it is the one that works fastest.

That masked email told you who it is. So call them. Text them. Most of the time this is not malice — it is a guy who forgot the listing existed, and he will approve it in about four seconds once he understands what you are asking for. Google's automated email went to an inbox he stopped checking in 2023.

I have resolved more of these with a phone call than with the form.

Step 4: When the seven days run out

If nobody responds, Google may let you claim the listing directly. You will get prompted to verify that you actually run the business, and these days that usually means video verification — you record yourself walking up to your sign, your truck, your equipment, proving the operation is real and you are the one running it.

A few things that make video verification go smoothly:

  • Do it in one unbroken take. No cuts, no edits.

  • Show something with your business name on it that is not a printout — a truck wrap, a permanent sign, an embroidered shirt.

  • Show your actual tools or equipment.

  • Show your face.

  • Do not film it from your couch.

If you are a service-area business with no storefront, this still works fine. Your truck and your gear are the proof.

Step 5: Get back in, then lock the door behind you

Once you have access, spend ten more minutes doing the part that keeps this from happening again:

  • Make yourself Primary Owner, not just a manager.

  • Add a second owner you trust — your spouse, your ops person — on an email address the business actually controls.

  • Remove every old user still sitting on the account.

  • Turn on notifications so you see reviews and messages when they land.

The second-owner step is the one people skip and regret. If you ever lose access to your own account, a second owner is the difference between a two-minute phone call and doing all of this over again.

How long this actually takes

Fastest I have seen was two days, because the previous owner picked up the phone. Slowest was about six weeks, with a denied appeal and a re-verification stacked on top. Most land somewhere around two to three weeks.

The seven-day waiting period is the floor. Nothing gets you past it.

When to just do it yourself

Most people reading this can handle it alone, and honestly you should. It is free, it is your listing, and the process above is the whole process.

Where it gets genuinely messy:

  • There are two listings for your business and they need to be merged

  • Your profile is also suspended, which is a separate problem stacked on this one

  • You have already been denied once

  • The person holding it is being difficult on purpose

That is when it makes sense to hand it off. I do this work for Houston businesses — you can read what that looks like on the Google Business Profile help page.

But if yours is the simple version, go start it right now. Twenty minutes, and it is probably the highest-value twenty minutes you will spend on marketing this month.
